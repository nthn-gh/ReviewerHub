I. Verbs

What Is a Verb?

A verb is a word that tells about an action, condition, or state.

Examples of Verbs

runjumpwritesingisareseemsmellfeeltaste

Verbs Have Different Functions

Some verbs show an action, while others help another verb, connect the subject to information about it, or describe what we experience through our senses. In this reviewer, we'll explore all these verb types!

II–IV. Helping Verbs

What Is a Helping Verb?

A helping verb works together with a main verb to express an action, condition, or time. Also called auxiliary verbs.

Common Helping Verbs

amisarewaswerehashavehaddodoesdid
III. How to Identify a Helping Verb

Look for a verb that is helping another verb in the sentence.

Example
Maria is reading a book.
is = helping verb  •  reading = main verb
Another Example
The students were preparing their projects.
were = helping verb  •  preparing = main verb
IV. Common Helping Verbs in Action

AM

I am studying.

IS

She is writing.

ARE

They are playing.

WAS

He was sleeping.

WERE

We were talking.

HAS / HAVE / HAD

She has finished. They have completed. He had arrived early.

V–VIII. Linking Verbs

What Is a Linking Verb?

A linking verb connects the subject to a word or phrase that describes or identifies the subject. It does NOT show action on an object.

Example
Maria is happy.
Maria = subject  •  is = linking verb  •  happy = describes Maria

Forms of "Be"

amisarewaswerebebeingbeen

Other Linking Verbs

seembecomeappearremainfeellooksoundtastesmell
I am happy.

am connects I to happy.

The flowers were beautiful.

were connects flowers to beautiful.

The boy became sleepy.
VIII. Helping vs. Linking Verb

Helping Verb

She is singing.
"is" helps the verb "singing"

Linking Verb

She is happy.
"is" connects "she" to "happy"

Remember: Helping verb → helps another verb. Linking verb → connects the subject to information about it.

XVI–XXIII. Transitive & Intransitive

Direct Objects & Verb Types

XVI. Transitive Verb

A transitive verb needs a direct object to complete its meaning. The action is performed by the subject on something or someone.

Subject + Verb + Object
Example
Ana opened the door.

Opened what? → the door. Therefore "opened" is transitive.

The boy kicked the ball.
Maria read the book.
She bought a new bag.
The teacher checked the papers.
XIX–XX. Intransitive Verbs

An intransitive verb does not need a direct object. The action stands on its own.

The baby slept.

Slept what? → No direct object.

The birds flew.
The children laughed.
The dog barked.

Transitive

Needs a direct object.
"The boy kicked the ball."

Intransitive

No direct object.
"The boy slept."

XXII–XXIII. Same Verb, Different Uses
Twisted — Transitive
She twisted the rope.
Twisted — Intransitive
The rope twisted.

XXX–XXXVII. Filling Out Forms

What Is a Form?

A form is a document that asks for information, used to collect data in an organized way.

Examples of Forms

Personal information sheetsRegistration formsSchool formsApplication formsPermission slips
XXXI. Steps to Fill Out a Form Accurately
1

Read the labels carefully

Understand what information is being requested.

2

Write the correct information

Do not guess.

3

Follow the required format

Some fields need a specific format (e.g. dates).

4

Check your spelling

Make sure names and places are spelled correctly.

5

Review before submitting

Look for missing or incorrect information.

XXXVIII–XLI. Summary

Important Terms & Quick Memory Guide

HELPING VERB
A verb that works with another verb (auxiliary verb).
LINKING VERB
Connects the subject to information describing or identifying it.
SENSE VERB
A verb related to one of the five senses.
TRANSITIVE VERB
A verb that takes a direct object.
INTRANSITIVE VERB
A verb that does not take a direct object.
DIRECT OBJECT
The person or thing receiving the action of a transitive verb.
FORM
A document used to collect information.
PERSONAL INFORMATION
Details about a person: name, age, address, date of birth.
